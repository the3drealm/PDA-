Quick goals: the course in plain words

In one line: a plant produces lots of messy data. You clean it, look at it, build a model from it, then use the model to watch the plant and make it run better.

Unit 1: What data is

Idea: data is like crude oil: useless until processed.

  • Name the types of data with a plant example each (number or label, countable or continuous, nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio).
  • Explain the four kinds of analytics: what happened, why, what will happen, what should we do.
  • Tell supervised (has answers) from unsupervised (no answers, finds groups).
  • Draw the data lifecycle: generate → collect → store → clean → analyse → model → decide → monitor.

Unit 2: Cleaning data

Idea: raw data has gaps, spikes and noise, so fix it before using it.

  • Explain DCS (controls one plant), SCADA (watches a wide area) and the historian (stores years of data).
  • Fill missing values: use interpolation when the data is trending.
  • Find outliers with the z-score, and know it can fail because the outlier distorts the average.
  • Do the scaling sums: min–max, z-score, decimal scaling, and binning.
  • Explain feature selection: filter (fast score), wrapper (try many models), embedded (lasso).

Unit 3: Looking at data

Idea: summarise with numbers and plots before modelling.

  • Calculate mean, median, mode, SD, CV, quartiles and fences, then draw a box plot.
  • Say which way data is skewed, and report the median when it is.
  • Calculate correlation r and say what it means (it shows a link, not a cause).
  • Explain PCA: it squeezes many related variables into a few new ones. Work out the percentage of variance from eigenvalues.

Unit 4: Models

Idea: learn a rule from past data to predict new data.

  • Fit a straight line, find R² and adjusted R², and make a prediction.
  • Explain ridge (shrinks coefficients) and lasso (shrinks and drops some).
  • Explain logistic regression, k-NN and decision trees, and calculate entropy and information gain.
  • Explain K-means and hierarchical clustering with a flowchart and a dendrogram.
  • Do the exponential smoothing sum and say what p, d, q mean in ARIMA.
  • Draw a neuron and say how a network learns (guess, measure error, adjust weights, repeat).

Unit 5: Watching the plant

Idea: separate normal wobble from a real problem.

  • Draw a control chart and calculate x̄ and R chart limits.
  • Tell common cause (natural) from special cause (something went wrong).
  • Explain T² (far from normal but behaving consistently) and SPE (the usual pattern is broken).
  • Explain the monitoring loop: detect → identify → diagnose → recover.
  • Compare reactive, preventive and predictive maintenance.

Unit 6: Using it in industry

Idea: put the models to work safely.

  • Define a soft sensor (estimates a slow lab value from fast sensors) and list the steps to build one.
  • Explain a digital twin (a live virtual copy of the plant).
  • Give two applications each for reactions, energy and environment.
  • List the ethics points and the cybersecurity measures.

  2. Practise the diagrams: almost every theory question wants one.
  3. Learn the comparison tables: DCS vs SCADA, ridge vs lasso, K-means vs hierarchical, common vs special cause, and the three maintenance types.

Unit 1 · Introduction

Definitions to write word for word

  • Data: recorded values of an experiment or survey taken together; each value is an observation.
  • Data analysis: cleaning, transforming and modelling data to discover useful information and support decisions.
  • Machine learning: a computer learns the input–output relationship from historical data instead of being given explicit rules.

Data as the new raw material (four grounds)

Produced continuously and in bulk. Little value until processed, like crude oil. Converted into products: soft sensors, alarms, set points, maintenance schedules. Creates economic value: less off-spec, energy, downtime, accidents.

Challenges of industrial data (any three or four)

Volume and velocity. Data quality (missing, frozen, spikes, drift, noise). Different sampling rates. Collinearity. Non-stationarity (fouling, catalyst decay). Few labelled faults. Time delays. Missing context.

Interval has an arbitrary zero, so 40 °C is not twice 20 °C. Ratio has a true zero. The syllabus trio: continuous (sensor signals), categorical (states and labels), time-series (ordered in time, autocorrelated).

Four types of analytics

Supervised and unsupervised learning

Supervised: inputs X and known output y; learn y = f(X). Regression (continuous y) and classification (categorical y). Example: predict yield from T, P, flow. Unsupervised: X only; find structure. Clustering, PCA, anomaly detection. Example: group compressor data into operating modes. Reinforcement learning learns a policy by trial and reward.

Data lifecycle (eight blocks plus feedback)

Generation → acquisition → storage → cleaning → analysis (EDA) → model development → decision making → monitoring, with feedback to generation. For the CSTR trace: thermocouple and 4–20 mA transmitter; DCS samples each second; historian archives with compression; engineer removes calibration periods and aligns with lab data; scatter plot shows the link to conversion; regression model; set point moved 80 → 83 °C; control chart on residuals and retraining.

Reactive to proactive: soft sensors give quality every minute, multivariate monitoring catches small correlated deviations, predictive maintenance schedules repairs, forecasting anticipates loads.

DCS, SCADA, historian

  • DCS: network of microprocessor modules doing real-time control of one plant from a central control room. Parts: I/O modules, local I/O bus, controller module (the brain), communication module, redundant data highway (the backbone), operator and engineering workstations.
  • SCADA: acquires data over a wide area and lets operators supervise. Parts: RTUs at remote sites, communication medium, master terminal unit, HMI. Scan interval must be shorter than the fastest significant change.

Historian data-quality problems (problem → detection)

Gaps → count NaN or Bad flags. Frozen values → rolling SD near zero. Spikes → Hampel filter or rate-of-change limit. Drift → compare with redundant sensor or a balance. Saturation → values piled at range limit. Noise → high-frequency fluctuation. Time-stamp errors → cross-correlation lags.

Cleaning pipeline (Fig 2.5, eleven steps)

Understand tags and units → remove duplicates, fix types and time stamps → remove shutdown, start-up, manual periods → flag invalid values → treat outliers → handle missing values → filter noise → align and resample → transform → scale → feature engineering and selection.

Why align and resample: algorithms need one row per time instant; a lab sample must be matched with conditions shifted by dead time; down-sampling reduces noise while up-sampling invents values; clock offsets destroy correlation.

Transformations and encoding

Log (Arrhenius ln k, skewed data). Binning (temperature Low/Normal/High). Aggregation (1 s steam flow to daily consumption). Encoding (catalyst A/B/C). Label encoding A→0, B→1, C→2 implies a false order for nominal data. One-hot makes one binary column per category and is preferred for nominal variables in distance-based or linear models. Label encoding suits ordinal variables and trees.

Missing data

Mechanisms: MCAR (random drop-outs), MAR (depends on other observed variables), MNAR (depends on the value itself, e.g. saturated transmitter). Methods: delete rows, delete the tag, mean or median, forward fill (set points, lab values), linear interpolation (short gaps in smooth series), spline, regression from correlated tags, k-NN. Never interpolate across a long shutdown.

Outliers

Detect: plots; z-score |z| > 3; modified z-score M = 0.6745(x − median)/MAD, flag |M| > 3.5; IQR rule; Hampel filter; physical and rate-of-change limits; multivariate (Mahalanobis, T², SPE). Treat: investigate first, delete if an error, replace by interpolation or local median, cap (winsorise), log transform, keep genuine events and model separately, use robust methods.

Noise

Sources: turbulence and pulsation, electrical interference, vibration, A/D quantisation, analyser and lab error. Reduce by binning, moving average, EWMA filter x̃t = αxt + (1−α)x̃t−1, median filter (removes spikes, keeps steps), Savitzky–Golay, averaging redundant sensors.

Scaling

Needed because k-NN, K-means, SVM, PCA, gradient training and ridge/lasso are dominated by large-number variables. Trees do not need it. Min–max is very sensitive to outliers. Z-score is preferred for PCA, regression, k-NN. Robust scaling uses median and IQR. Scaling never changes the shape. Fit the scaler on training data only, otherwise data leakage.

Features

Feature engineering: domain knowledge creates better inputs. Reflux ratio L/D, ΔT, heat duty, ΔP, residence time V/Q, U = Q/(AΔTlm) for fouling, 1/T, lags, rolling mean and SD, time since catalyst change, vibration RMS and FFT bands.

Selection vs extraction: selection keeps a subset of original variables and their meaning; extraction (PCA, PLS) builds new combinations and usually loses interpretability.

Unit 3 · Exploratory data analysis

EDA (Tukey, 1977): first look at cleaned data with summary statistics and graphs to understand distributions, spot errors and outliers, find relationships and form hypotheses before modelling.

Descriptive statistics

  • Centre: mean (uses every value, sensitive to outliers), median (robust, for skewed data), mode (only average for nominal data; two modes can mean two operating modes).
  • Spread: range, variance, SD, CV = s/x̄ × 100 (compares variables with different means), IQR = Q3 − Q1.
  • Sample variance divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) because one degree of freedom goes to estimating x̄.
  • Quartiles: position of Qk is k(n+1)/4; interpolate when not an integer. State the rule.
  • Fences: Q1 − 1.5 IQR and Q3 + 1.5 IQR. Whiskers go to the last points inside the fences, not to the fences.
  • Shape: right skew has Mode < Median < Mean; left skew the reverse. The mean is pulled toward the long tail. Pearson Sk = 3(x̄ − median)/s. Kurtosis: tail heaviness, normal = 3.
  • Normal rule: 68.3%, 95.4%, 99.73% within 1, 2, 3 σ.

Covariance and correlation

Covariance has units; r is dimensionless, between −1 and 1, and measures linear association only. Strength: 0.9–1 very strong, 0.7–0.9 strong, 0.4–0.7 moderate, 0.2–0.4 weak. r = +0.92: move together, one input may be redundant. r = −0.85: one rises as the other falls. r = 0.05: no linear link, but check the scatter plot for a curve. Correlation is not causation.

Plots and what each reveals

  • Histogram: distribution shape, skew, multiple peaks. Bins: Sturges k ≈ 1 + 3.3 log₁₀ n.
  • Box plot: median, spread, skewness, outliers; side by side compares shifts or batches.
  • Scatter plot: form, direction and strength of a relationship, clusters, outliers.
  • Trend plot: drift, cycles, steps, shutdowns, frozen sensors.
  • Correlation heat map: find inputs related to quality, find collinear groups, spot a sensor that disagrees with its twin.

PCA

Transforms p correlated variables into uncorrelated principal components, linear combinations ordered by variance captured.

  1. Arrange X (n × p).
  2. Standardise each variable.
  3. Covariance matrix S = XᵀX/(n−1).
  4. Eigen-decomposition Sp = λp. Eigenvalue = variance of that PC; eigenvector = loadings.
  5. Sort; compute λj/Σλ and cumulative.
  6. Choose k: scree elbow, cumulative 80–90%, Kaiser λ > 1.
  7. Scores T = XPk.
  8. Interpret score and loading plots; residuals E = X − TPᵀ.

Score plot: each point is an observation. Clusters are operating modes, far points are abnormal, slow drift is fouling or catalyst decay. Loading plot: each point is a variable. Far from origin means important; close together means positively correlated; opposite sides means negatively correlated.

Advantages: removes collinearity, reduces dimension, filters noise, basis for T² and SPE. Limits: linear, hard to interpret, sensitive to scaling and outliers, ignores the output.

Unit 4 · Statistical and ML techniques

Linear regression

b₁ = Sxy/Sxx, b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄. SST = SSR + SSE. R² = 1 − SSE/SST; for one predictor R² = r². Multiple regression: b̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy; each coefficient is the change in y per unit xj with the others held constant. Adjusted R² penalises extra predictors, so use it to compare models of different size.

Assumptions: linearity, independent errors, constant variance (homoscedasticity), normal errors, no perfect multicollinearity. Check with residual plots: random band is good; curve means a missing non-linear term; funnel means heteroscedasticity, try log y.

Multicollinearity: strong correlation among predictors. XᵀX nearly singular, unstable coefficients and sign flips, inflated standard errors. Detect with |r| > 0.8–0.9 or VIF = 1/(1 − Rj²) above 5–10. Fix by removing or combining variables, ridge, PCR, PLS.

Why lasso selects: its constraint region is a diamond with corners on the axes, and the SSE ellipses usually touch a corner, where one coefficient is zero. Ridge's circle has no corners. Ridge handles multicollinearity because adding λI makes XᵀX invertible. λ is chosen by cross-validation; standardise first. Overfitting: training error far below test error. k-fold CV: train on k−1 parts, test on one, rotate, average.

Classification

  • Logistic regression: P(y=1) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᶻ), z = b₀ + Σbjxj. Log-odds are linear. Fitted by maximum likelihood. Predict pass if p ≥ 0.5 (or a stricter threshold). Linear decision boundary.
  • k-NN: choose k and distance; compute distance to every training point; take the k nearest; majority vote. Small k: high variance, overfits. Large k: smooth, underfits. Odd k for two classes. Scaling essential.
  • Decision tree: root node, internal nodes, branches, leaves. Each split chosen to make children purest. Entropy H = −Σp log₂p, Gini = 1 − Σp², information gain = H(parent) − weighted H(children). ID3/C4.5 use gain, CART uses Gini. Easy to interpret, no scaling, but overfits; control with depth and pruning.

Confusion matrix: accuracy = (TP+TN)/N, precision = TP/(TP+FP), recall = TP/(TP+FN), F1 = 2PR/(P+R). With rare faults accuracy misleads.

Clustering

K-means: choose K and standardise → initialise K centroids → assign each point to the nearest centroid → recompute centroids as cluster means → repeat until assignments stop changing. Minimises WCSS. Limitations: K fixed in advance, depends on initial centroids, assumes spherical clusters, sensitive to outliers and scaling.

Elbow method: plot WCSS against K and pick the bend. Other method: silhouette s = (b − a)/max(a, b), highest average wins.

Agglomerative: each point its own cluster → merge the two closest → update distances by linkage → repeat; record in a dendrogram. Linkage: single (closest pair, chains), complete (farthest pair, compact), average, Ward (least increase in within-cluster variance). Cut the dendrogram horizontally; lines crossed = clusters.

K-means vs hierarchical: K fixed first vs chosen after; flat partition vs dendrogram; fast vs slow O(n²); depends on initialisation vs deterministic; points can move vs merges are permanent.

Time series

Components: trend, seasonality (fixed period), cyclic (no fixed period), irregular. Additive y = T + S + C + I; multiplicative y = T × S × C × I. Stationary: mean, variance and autocorrelation constant in time. ARMA needs it, so differencing ∇yt = yt − yt−1 removes a trend and a seasonal difference removes seasonality. Check with ACF and the ADF test.

ARIMA(p, d, q): p = autoregressive order (lagged values), d = number of differences, q = moving-average order (lagged errors). PACF cuts off after p for AR; ACF cuts off after q for MA. Box–Jenkins: make stationary → identify → estimate → check residuals are white noise → forecast.

Exponential smoothing: Ft+1 = αyt + (1−α)Ft. Large α reacts fast but passes noise. Holt adds trend, Holt–Winters adds seasonality. ARIMA models autocorrelation, needs stationarity and longer series; smoothing is weighted averaging, simple, fine for short series.

Neural networks

Neuron: z = Σwjxj + b, output a = f(z). Weights are the learned strengths; bias shifts the activation like an intercept; activation (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU) adds non-linearity, without which layers collapse to a linear model. Feed-forward: input layer → hidden layer(s) → output, no loops.

Training: initialise small random weights → forward pass → loss (MSE or cross-entropy) → backpropagation computes gradients by the chain rule → gradient descent w ← w − η∂L/∂w → repeat over epochs until validation loss stops falling. Learning rate too large diverges, too small crawls. Networks need much data and extrapolate badly.

Unit 5 · Monitoring and fault detection

SPC and control charts

SPC: statistical methods, chiefly control charts, to monitor a process, separate natural variation from assignable causes and trigger action before off-spec product is made (Shewhart, 1920s).

Limits: CL = μ, UCL and LCL = μ ± 3σ. False-alarm chance 0.27% per point, ARL₀ ≈ 370. x̄ chart tracks level, R chart tracks spread; check R first. Control limits come from the process, specification limits from the customer. CUSUM and EWMA charts catch small sustained shifts faster.

Western Electric rules: (1) one point beyond 3σ; (2) two of three consecutive in zone A or beyond, same side; (3) four of five in zone B or beyond, same side; (4) eight consecutive on one side of the centre line. Zones: C 0–1σ, B 1–2σ, A 2–3σ.

Why univariate charts fail

Missed faults: a fault can break the correlation between variables while each stays inside its own limits (inside the rectangle, outside the ellipse). False alarms: with p charts the overall rate is 1 − 0.9973ᵖ, 2.7% for 10 and 13% for 50.

T² and SPE with PCA

Off-line: collect normal operating data → standardise → build PCA with k components → set limits for T² and SPE. On-line: standardise each new sample, compute scores, then both statistics.

  • Hotelling's T² = Σ ta²/λa: distance from the centre within the model plane. High T² means correlation intact but operation unusually far from normal.
  • SPE (Q) = Σ(xj − x̂j)²: distance from the plane. High SPE means the correlation structure is broken: sensor failure, leak, new disturbance.
  • Contribution plot: bar chart of each variable's share, cj = (xj − x̂j)². Largest bars point to the variables involved. They show symptoms and do not prove the cause.

Monitoring loop

Fault detection (is something abnormal?) → fault identification (which variables?) → fault diagnosis (root cause, where, how big?) → process recovery, then monitoring continues. A fault is an unpermitted deviation of at least one characteristic property from its acceptable condition.

Anomaly detection

Types: point, contextual, collective. Approaches: statistical thresholds (EWMA chart on pH catches a failing dosing pump); multivariate (PCA SPE on an exchanger network); model-residual (predicted vs measured compressor discharge temperature); distance or density (k-NN, LOF, DBSCAN); machine learning (isolation forest, autoencoder reconstruction error).

AI/ML in safety and reliability

Early warning of abnormal situations. Alarm management during floods. Predictive maintenance of safety-critical equipment. Leak, gas and fire detection by computer vision. Text mining of incident reports. Human-error detection. Risk and consequence surrogates. Corrosion and integrity. ML supports engineered safety layers and never replaces them.

Unit 6 · Industrial applications

Soft sensors

A model that estimates a variable that is difficult, expensive or slow to measure from variables measured easily and quickly. Why: real-time estimates between lab samples, back-up for the analyser, check on analyser drift, lower cost.

Types: model-driven (white-box, balances and thermodynamics; extrapolates well, costly), data-driven (black-box; MLR, PCR, PLS, neural networks), hybrid (grey-box).

Development (Fig 6.1): define target and accuracy → collect process and lab data → pre-process and align lab with lagged inputs → select inputs → train → validate on an unseen period → deploy in DCS or historian → monitor and update (bias correction, retraining). Example: purity from trays 5, 12, 18, top pressure, reflux ratio, feed rate using PLS. Main issue: model degradation.

Optimisation with ML

Data-driven operating windows. Surrogate-model optimisation: train ŷ = f(x), then maximise subject to constraints. Bayesian optimisation for expensive experiments. Real-time optimisation feeding MPC. Reinforcement learning. Precautions: keep the search inside the training range, confirm the optimum with a mechanistic model or plant trial, respect safety limits.

Real-time analytics (Fig 6.2)

Sensors and IIoT → DCS/PLC/edge gateway → historian or data lake (streaming) → analytics engine (soft sensors, monitoring, forecasts) → decision support (dashboards, alerts) → operator, and APC/MPC/RTO sending set points back. Digital twin: a continuously updated virtual replica of the plant used for what-if studies, operator training, monitoring and optimisation without risk. Control hierarchy: PID (seconds) → MPC (minutes) → RTO (hours) → planning (days).

Applications (two each)

  • Reaction engineering: kinetic parameter estimation from ln k vs 1/T; yield prediction; catalyst deactivation monitoring; golden-batch analytics; runaway detection.
  • Energy: steam and utility optimisation; boiler efficiency; exchanger fouling by tracking U; specific energy baselining.
  • Environmental: ETP soft sensors for BOD and COD; predictive emission monitoring (PEMS); leak detection and repair; aeration control.

Ethics

Safety first. Transparency and explainability. Accountability stays with people (management of change applies to models). Data integrity (ALCOA: attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate). Privacy and workforce. Bias and representativeness. Data ownership.

Cybersecurity

Connecting OT to IT opens attack paths; consequences are loss of control, damage, releases, injuries. Incidents: Stuxnet (2010, PLCs), Triton (2017, safety system), Colonial Pipeline (2021, ransomware). Measures: network segmentation with a DMZ and one-way data flow, IEC 62443 and NIST framework, access control and multi-factor authentication, patching and hardening, OT traffic monitoring and backups, independent safety system, staff training.

Smart manufacturing

Industry 4.0: cyber-physical systems, IIoT, cloud and edge, analytics, AI, digital twins. Case study to quote: a PLS soft sensor for top purity used as the controlled variable in MPC gives tighter purity control and less reflux and reboiler energy. Second: compressor predictive maintenance, where rising SPE with one bearing's vibration dominant led to a planned bearing change.
